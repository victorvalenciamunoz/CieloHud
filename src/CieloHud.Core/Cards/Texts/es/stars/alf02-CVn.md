# Cor Caroli

Su nombre latino significa «el corazón de Carlos», por el rey Carlos I de Inglaterra, decapitado en 1649, y no por
Carlos II, como se suele decir. Con un telescopio se ve doble. La más brillante es el modelo de un tipo de estrellas
magnéticas: su campo es más de 2000 veces más fuerte que el de la Tierra, y tiene grandes manchas que aparecen y
desaparecen al girar, cada 5,5 días.

## Fuentes

- Jim Kaler, Stars, Cor Caroli (stars.astro.illinois.edu/sow/corcaroli.html): «The telescope quickly reveals that Cor Caroli is double»; «Alpha-2 is the prototype "magnetic star" that defines a class of "Alpha-2 Canum Venaticorum stars"»; «Cor Caroli's, on the other hand, has one more than 2000 times stronger than our planet's»; «the enhanced elements (and magnetic fields) are concentrated into huge long-lasting "starspots" ... as the star rotates, the spots swing into and out of view»; «Alpha-2 rotates with a period of 5.46939 days».
- Ian Ridpath, Star Tales, Canes Venatici (ianridpath.com/startales/canesvenatici.html): «Alpha Canum Venaticorum, popularly known as Cor Caroli, meaning Charles's Heart, in honour of King Charles I of England who was beheaded by parliamentarians in 1649»; «in 1844 the English astronomer William Henry Smyth started a long-running confusion when he wrote in his Bedford Catalogue that the star had been named by Edmond Halley after King Charles II, a mistake originally due to Bode in his star catalogue of 1801»; «Sherburne made clear that it referred to the first King Charles».
