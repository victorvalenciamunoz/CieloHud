# Erídano

El río del cielo, en el que cayó Faetón, el hijo del Sol, cuando no supo llevar el carro de su padre y Zeus lo derribó con
un rayo. Eratóstenes decía que era el Nilo, aunque el del cielo corre de norte a sur. Para Ptolomeo acababa en Acamar; a
finales del siglo XVI se alargó hasta Achernar, «el final del río», una de las estrellas más brillantes, que él no podía
ver desde tan al norte.

## Fuentes

- Ian Ridpath, Star Tales, Eridanus (ianridpath.com/startales/eridanus.html): «the river Eridanus features in the story of Phaethon, son of the Sun-god Helios, who begged to be allowed to drive his father's chariot across the sky»; «Even had Phaethon known where the true path lay, he lacked the skill and the strength to control the reins»; «Zeus struck Phaethon down with a thunderbolt [...] the youth plunged like a shooting star into the Eridanus»; «Eratosthenes had another identification: he said that the constellation represented the Nile»; «the celestial river is visualized as flowing from north to south, opposite to the direction of the real Nile»; «Achernar, from the Arabic ākhir al-nahr meaning 'the river's end'»; «In Ptolemy's day, though, the river dried up 17° farther north, at the star to which Johann Bayer assigned the Greek letter Theta [...] Theta Eridani was then renamed Acamar»; «Eridanus was first shown flowing southwards to the present-day Alpha Eridani on a globe of 1598»; «The present-day Achernar is the only first-magnitude star not listed in Ptolemy's Almagest, because it was too far south for him to see».
