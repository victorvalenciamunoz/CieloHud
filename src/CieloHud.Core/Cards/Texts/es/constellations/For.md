# Horno

Un horno de químico para destilar, otra invención de Lacaille tras observar el cielo del sur en 1751-1752. Está metido en
una curva del río Erídano. Se suele decir que lo dedicó a Lavoisier, uno de los fundadores de la química, pero Lavoisier
tenía entonces 13 años: fue Bode quien, casi medio siglo después, lo redibujó como su experimento para descomponer el agua
en hidrógeno y oxígeno.

## Fuentes

- Ian Ridpath, Star Tales, Fornax (ianridpath.com/startales/fornax.html): «introduced by the Frenchman Nicolas Louis de Lacaille after his trip to the Cape of Good Hope to observe the southern stars in 1751–52, depicting a chemist's furnace used for distillation. It lies tucked into a bend in the river Eridanus»; «It is sometimes said that Lacaille invented the constellation to honour his countryman Antoine Lavoisier, one of the founders of chemistry. This is a misunderstanding, since Lavoisier was only 13 years old when Lacaille's chart of the southern constellations was first published. In fact, the connection with Lavoisier was due to Johann Bode who reinvented the constellation nearly half a century later»; «based on Lavoisier's diagram of his experiment to decompose water into its constituents of hydrogen and oxygen».
